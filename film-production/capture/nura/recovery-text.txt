Understand your recovery score
What recovery tells you about your body

Recovery reads how settled your nervous system was overnight. When training, short sleep, alcohol, stress or an oncoming illness has taxed your body, heart rate stays higher and beat-to-beat variation (HRV) drops. When you have absorbed the load, the opposite happens.

Training hard on repeated low-recovery days tends to add fatigue without adding fitness. Matching effort to recovery is how you improve without running yourself down.

How your recovery score is built

Each signal is compared with your own last 28 nights, never with other people:

HRV, 24%. Higher than your usual is better.
Sleeping heart rate, 29%. Lower than your usual is better.
Sleep against your need, 29%. Meeting the need is better.
Breathing rate, 12% and wrist temperature, 6%. Staying near your usual is better; a rise in both often comes before feeling ill.

67 and above is green, 34 to 66 yellow, under 34 red. If two or more signals are far off on the same night, the score is capped at 33 so one good number cannot hide a rough night.

What makes it good
A full, unbroken night at your regular hours.
Easy days or rest after hard sessions.
Staying hydrated and eating enough on training days.
Aerobic fitness built over months: it lifts HRV and lowers sleeping heart rate.
What makes it bad
Alcohol. Even two drinks commonly raises sleeping heart rate and cuts HRV that night.
Very hard or very late training, and several hard days in a row.
Short or broken sleep, travel across time zones, mental stress.
Illness: watch for higher breathing rate and wrist temperature together.
